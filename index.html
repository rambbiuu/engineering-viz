What a radio link is, and how a wave gets anywhere

Bands and how radio travels

Why AM reaches another country at night and Wi-Fi does not reach the next room. The eight bands, the wavelength that goes with each, and the three paths a wave can take — ground wave, off the ionosphere, or straight to the horizon.

Link budget

The one sum every radio link comes down to. Transmit power, antenna gain, the loss the distance takes, and whether what arrives still stands clear of the noise floor — with the horizon marked, because free space is not the whole story.

What a signal is actually made of

Time and frequency

The same signal drawn as a shape over time and as the list of sine waves it is built from. Add them one at a time and watch a square wave appear — then see why a sharper edge, or a shorter pulse, always costs you bandwidth.

Putting a message on a carrier

AM, double and single sideband, FM and PM. Watch the message ride the carrier in time, then see where it went in frequency — sidebands beside the carrier, and how much of your power is wasted holding a carrier that says nothing.

Antennas

Where the energy goes, and how to point it

Antenna beam pattern

Where an antenna actually sends its energy. Dipole, monopole, collinear, Yagi, log-periodic or horn — drawn side on and top down, with the vertical and horizontal cut each one produces.

Phased-array beam pattern

The same array run in reverse: feed each element a phase shift and steer a beam. Beamwidth, sidelobes, grating lobes, and why sparse arrays trade cleanliness for sharpness.

Polarisation

A wave’s electric field points somewhere, and an antenna only takes the part lined up with it. Turn one 45° and half the power is gone; turn it 90° and all of it is. Linear, slanted and circular, with the loss for every combination.

Finding an emitter

Three ways to work out where a signal came from

Phase interferometry

How an unequally spaced antenna array turns phase differences into an angle of arrival — sky view, scope view, wrapping and where the ambiguity comes from.

AoA triangulation

Two to five sites each measure a bearing. Cross them and they meet at the emitter — and see how an angle error grows into a distance error with range.

TDoA multilateration

Three to five clocks, no bearings. Each pair of sites draws a curve of possible positions, and the curves cross at the emitter — with 300 m of error for every microsecond of clock slip.
