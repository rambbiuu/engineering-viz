Erlang B, blocking and trunking efficiency

A cell has a fixed number of radio channels and no idea how many people will call at once. Erlang B turns that into one number: the chance a caller finds every channel busy.

    Assumptions, and what breaks them

    Calls arrive at random from a large population (Poisson), call lengths are exponential about mean H, and a blocked caller gives up and never retries. That last one is what makes it Erlang B; queue the blocked callers instead and the answer is Erlang C. Pooled supermarket tills serve more shoppers than the same tills in separate queues, because the rushes cancel — radio channels do the same.

    Glossary
    Erlang (Er)
    Traffic intensity. One erlang is one channel occupied continuously for the hour. A time divided by a time, so it has no units.
    Offered traffic A
    The traffic callers try to place: arrival rate times mean holding time, A = λH. It includes the calls that will be blocked.
    Carried traffic
    The traffic that actually gets a channel, A(1 − Pb). Always lower than offered.
    Grade of service
    The blocking probability a network is designed to meet in its busiest hour. 2 % is common; 1 % is generous.
    Holding time H
    Mean length of a call. Erlang B assumes call lengths follow an exponential distribution about this mean.
    Trunk group
    A set of channels any caller in the area can take. Erlang B applies to one trunk group at a time.
    Trunking efficiency
    A large trunk group runs at much higher utilisation than several small ones at the same grade of service.
    Blocked calls cleared
    The caller who finds everything busy is lost, with no queue and no retry. The assumption behind Erlang B.
    Sectorisation
    Splitting a cell into directional wedges. It cuts interference, but splits one trunk group into several smaller ones.
    Cluster size N
    The licence is divided N ways, so each cell gets a fraction. Only certain N exist: 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 12, 13, 19, 21.
