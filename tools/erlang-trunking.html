Erlang B, blocking and trunking efficiency

A cell has a fixed number of radio channels and no idea how many people will call at once. Erlang B turns that gamble into one number: the chance a caller finds every channel busy and gets nothing.

New to cells and channel groups? Start with frequency reuse, which explains where a cell's channel count comes from.

Pooled supermarket checkouts serve far more shoppers than the same tills in separate queues, because the rushes cancel — radio channels do the same.

Assumptions, and they are not decoration: calls arrive at random from a large population (Poisson), call lengths are exponentially distributed with mean H, and a blocked caller gives up and never retries. That last one is what makes it Erlang B; queue the blocked callers instead and the answer is Erlang C.

Worked example — sizing a 30-channel cell at 2 % blocking
  1. Per-user traffic: A_u = λ_u · H = 2 calls/h × 120 s = 2 × 0.0333 h = 0.0667 Er. One erlang is one channel held continuously.
  2. Erlang B by the stable recursion, never factorials: B(A,0) = 1, then B(A,k) = A·B(A,k−1) / (k + A·B(A,k−1)) for k = 1 to C.
  3. There is no closed form for the inverse, so bisect on A until B(A,30) = 0.02. That gives A = 21.93 Er.
  4. Carried traffic = A(1 − B) = 21.93 × 0.98 = 21.49 Er. Utilisation = 21.49 / 30 = 71.6 %.
  5. Users supported = 21.93 / 0.0667 = 329. Halve the holding time and the same channels serve twice as many people.
  6. Check against the published table at 1 % blocking: C = 5 gives 1.3608 Er, C = 50 gives 37.9014 Er. Set the sliders there and the cards reproduce both.

Ten separate five-channel pools carry 10 × 1.3472 = 13.47 Er. One fifty-channel pool carries 37.52 Er, at the same grade of service, from the same fifty channels.

Three things fall out of these sliders. Doubling the channels far more than doubles the traffic, because a bigger pool absorbs its own rushes: one channel holds 0.0101 Er at 1 % blocking, fifty hold 37.90 Er, which is 3750 times the traffic from fifty times the hardware. Loosening the target from 1 % to 5 % buys real capacity from the same radios, which is why a network designed for a quiet suburb can be re-graded rather than rebuilt. And sectorising a cell chops one trunk group into three or six, sliding utilisation back down the curve, so it only pays when the interference it removes lets you shrink the cluster size N and hand each cell more channels in the first place.

Glossary
Erlang (Er)
Traffic intensity. One erlang is one channel occupied continuously for the hour. It has no units: it is a time divided by a time.
Offered traffic A
The traffic callers try to place: arrival rate times mean holding time, A = λH. It includes the calls that will be blocked.
Carried traffic
The traffic that actually gets a channel, A(1 − P_b). Always lower than offered. Erlang B tables list offered, so the two are easy to confuse.
Grade of service
The blocking probability a network is designed to meet in its busiest hour. 2 % is a common target; 1 % is a generous one.
Holding time H
Mean length of a call. Erlang B assumes call lengths follow an exponential distribution about this mean.
Trunk group
A set of channels any caller in the area can take. Erlang B applies to one trunk group at a time.
Trunking efficiency
The fact that a large trunk group runs at much higher utilisation than several small ones at the same grade of service.
Blocked calls cleared
The caller who finds everything busy is lost, with no queue and no retry. The assumption behind Erlang B.
Sectorisation
Splitting a cell into directional wedges. It cuts interference, but it also splits one trunk group into several smaller ones.
Reuse factor N
Cluster size. The operator's channel licence is divided N ways, so each cell gets a fraction of the total. Only certain N are possible: 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 12, 13, 19, 21.
