Guided tour

Press 1 to walk through a mismatched antenna, a length of cable, and the two parts that fix it. Or move any slider yourself.

1 · What a mismatch actually looks like — the wave on the cable, with the reflected part drawn coming back

  • The blue line is the voltage on the cable at this instant. The dashed outline is how far it swings at each point along the cable.
  • Where the outgoing wave and the returning wave add you get a peak, Vmax. Where they cancel you get a dip, Vmin. Their ratio is the VSWR.
  • A flat outline means nothing is coming back. The more the outline bulges, the worse the match.

2 · The Smith chart — every impedance in the world, folded into one circle; drag the point to move the antenna

  • The outer circle is total reflection. The centre is a perfect match, where the antenna looks exactly like Z0.
  • The faint circles are constant resistance and the faint arcs are constant reactance, both counted in units of Z0. The chart is a map of impedance, not of position.
  • The orange dot is the antenna itself. The blue dot is what the transmitter finally sees. The dashed circle through the blue dot is a circle of constant VSWR.
  • Each part you fit draws one arc: a series part slides along a resistance circle, a shunt part slides along a conductance circle.

Drag anywhere inside the chart to move the antenna impedance. The R and X sliders follow, and stop at their limits.

3 · The match across frequency — a match is only ever a match over a band; this is how wide that band is

  • The blue curve is VSWR against frequency. The dashed line at 2 is the specification most antennas are sold against.
  • The shaded band is where the match holds. Its width in megahertz is the match bandwidth card above.
  • A match that fights a large resistance ratio is a narrow spike; a gentle one is a broad dish. That is what the Q figure is measuring.
The arithmetic, with this antenna's numbers in it

    Two numbers are worth keeping permanently calibrated. Return loss and mismatch loss are not the same figure and not each other's negative: return loss describes the wave coming back, mismatch loss describes the power that failed to go in. A VSWR of 2.0 is 9.54 dB of return loss, which sounds alarming, and only 0.51 dB of mismatch loss, which is nothing — eleven per cent of the power turns round, yet the link gives up half a decibel. That is exactly why VSWR 2 is a routine, shippable antenna specification, and why a student who believes VSWR 2 costs 9.5 dB will over-design every match they ever build. The other lesson is in section 3: cable length moves the point around the chart without ever shrinking the VSWR circle, real parts shrink it, and the narrower the match the faster it falls off the band edge. Two simplifications are made here and should be named: the antenna is modelled as a fixed resistance in series with one reactive part, so that its reactance moves with frequency the way a real component would rather than staying frozen; and mismatch loss is the best case, because it ignores what the returned power does when it reaches the amplifier.

    Glossary
    Impedance Z, in ohms
    How much voltage a component demands for a given current, written R + jX. R is the part that absorbs power; X is the part that only stores and returns it.
    Reactance X, in ohms
    The storing part of impedance. Positive X behaves like an inductor, negative X like a capacitor.
    Characteristic impedance Z0, in ohms
    The impedance a cable wants to see at its far end. 50 ohm is standard for radio, 75 ohm for television and video.
    Normalised impedance z
    Impedance divided by Z0, so z = 1 means a perfect match. The Smith chart is drawn in these units, which is why one chart serves every system.
    Reflection coefficient Γ
    The fraction of the wave amplitude that bounces off the load, with a phase. Γ = (ZL − Z0) / (ZL + Z0).
    VSWR
    Voltage standing wave ratio: the biggest voltage swing on the cable divided by the smallest. 1 is perfect, and it never goes below 1.
    Return loss, in dB
    How far below the outgoing wave the reflection sits, quoted as a positive number. 20 dB return loss is a good match; 6 dB is a poor one.
    Mismatch loss, in dB
    How much of the transmitter's power never entered the antenna because it was reflected.
    Admittance Y, susceptance B
    The upside-down view: Y = 1 / Z, measured in siemens. Its storing part is the susceptance B. Parts fitted across the line add susceptance, which is why shunt parts are easier to think about in admittance.
    Series and shunt
    A series part sits in line with the signal and adds reactance. A shunt part sits across the line, from signal to ground, and adds susceptance.
    Electrical length, in wavelengths
    A cable's length measured in wavelengths rather than metres. Half a wavelength of cable takes the impedance once all the way round the chart and back to where it started.
    L-network
    The two-part matching network used here: one shunt part and one series part. Its Q, and therefore its bandwidth, is fixed by the resistance ratio it has to bridge.
    Q
    How sharply tuned something is. For an L-network bridging Rhigh to Rlow, Q = √(Rhigh/Rlow − 1), and the match holds over roughly f0/Q.
