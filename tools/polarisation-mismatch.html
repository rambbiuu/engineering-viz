Polarisation, tilt and axial ratio

Polarisation is the direction a wave wiggles in, and the antenna has one too. Tilt the two apart and power is thrown away before any other engineering starts.

New to antennas? Start with antenna basics for what a dipole is and what a beam looks like. Everything here happens in the plane across the beam, not along it.

An everyday analogy: a letterbox slot only takes a letter held the right way round — a circular wave spins as it flies, so it always half-fits.

Guided tour

Press 1 to start. Each step moves the controls for you and says what changed.

1 · The field vector and the antenna it has to fit — left, the link seen from the side; right, the same wave seen head-on

  • Left is the hardware. The transmitter has two elements at right angles: a vertical one (violet, amplitude B) and a horizontal one seen end-on (green, amplitude A). Feeding them different amplitudes and a phase difference is how any polarisation is made.
  • The coral rod at the far end is the receiving dipole, tilted by ψ. Circular receive antennas are drawn as a crossed pair, because that is what they are.
  • Right is the head-on view, looking back down the beam at the oncoming wave. The blue arrow is the electric field; its tip traces the pale blue path once per cycle.
  • A straight path means linear polarisation, a circle means circular, anything between is elliptical. The dashed line is the ellipse's long axis and τ is its tilt.

2 · The two halves against time — Ex = A cos ωt and Ey = B cos(ωt + δ)

  • Green is Ex, the horizontal half of the field; violet is Ey, the vertical half. Their heights are A and B.
  • The bar across the top measures δ, the phase difference: how far the violet peak sits from the green one, as a fraction of one cycle.
  • δ = 0 puts the peaks together and the tip in section 1 walks a straight line. δ = ±90° with equal heights makes a circle. Everything else is an ellipse.
  • The vertical line is the instant drawn in section 1; it appears twice because the two marked instants are one whole cycle apart and the field is identical at both.

3 · Loss against receive tilt — the same wave, the receiving antenna rotated through a right angle

  • The blue curve is the power thrown away by the mismatch, in dB. Zero at the top is a perfect fit; further down is worse.
  • The coral dot is the tilt you have set with the ψ slider.
  • A flat curve means the link does not care how the antenna is rotated. That, and only that, is what circular polarisation buys you.
  • The shaded band is where real antennas stop, 25 to 35 dB. The ideal formula's null is infinitely deep; no hardware is.

The arithmetic, on the numbers now on screen

    Three results are worth carrying away. A 45° mismatch between two linear antennas costs exactly 3.01 dB, and the cost then runs away fast: 6.02 dB at 60° and 21.19 dB at 85°, so the last few degrees of alignment are worth more than all the earlier ones. A linear antenna against a perfectly circular wave loses 3.01 dB at every rotation angle, which is the trade circular polarisation offers — a fixed 3 dB in exchange for never caring about orientation again, which is why satellite and GPS links use it. And a real circular antenna is specified by its axial ratio, because the peak-to-peak swing of that 3 dB figure, as the far end rotates, is equal to the axial ratio in dB: 3 dB of axial ratio swings the loss from 1.76 to 4.76 dB, so half the axial ratio is the number to budget for. The one place this tool flatters reality is the null: the formula gives infinite rejection at a right angle, while a real antenna manages 25 to 35 dB of cross-polar discrimination, limited by its own imperfection and by rain depolarising the wave on the way. Plan a frequency-reuse scheme on 30 dB, never on infinity.

    Glossary
    Polarisation
    The direction the electric field of a wave points, seen in the plane across the beam. It is set by the antenna that launched the wave.
    Linear, circular, elliptical
    The three shapes the field vector's tip can trace: a straight line, a circle, or an ellipse between the two.
    Ex and Ey
    The horizontal and vertical halves of the field. Any polarisation is two of these added together with some phase difference.
    Phase difference δ
    How far the vertical half lags or leads the horizontal half, as an angle. One whole cycle is 360°.
    Axial ratio (AR)
    The long axis of the ellipse divided by the short one, always 1 or more. In dB it is 20 log10(AR), because it is a ratio of field amplitudes, not powers. 0 dB is a perfect circle; a straight line is infinite.
    Tilt angle τ
    The angle of the ellipse's long axis, measured up from the horizontal.
    RHCP and LHCP
    Right- and left-hand circular polarisation: the two directions the field can rotate. Point your thumb along the direction the wave travels; the hand whose fingers curl the way the field turns names the sense.
    Polarisation loss factor (PLF)
    The fraction of the arriving power that the receiving antenna can accept, from 1 (perfect fit) down to 0 (completely crossed).
    Polarisation loss
    The same thing in decibels: −10 log10(PLF). A PLF of 0.5 is 3.01 dB of loss.
    Cross-polar discrimination (XPD)
    How much better an antenna hears the polarisation it wants than the one at right angles to it. Real systems get 25 to 35 dB, which is what lets two links share a frequency on opposite polarisations.
    Decibel (dB)
    A ratio on a logarithmic scale. 3 dB is a factor of two in power, 10 dB is a factor of ten, 20 dB is a factor of a hundred.
    Dipole
    The simplest antenna: a straight rod. It is linearly polarised along its own length, which is why tilting it matters.
