Handover, hysteresis and power control

A call has to be passed from one base station to the next while the car keeps moving, and the only evidence the network has is a signal level that is jumping about by several decibels. Switch too eagerly and the call bounces back and forth between the two masts; switch too late and it is gone. This tool drives a mobile between two base stations and lets you set the rules that decide when the switch happens.

If dBm and margins are new, start with Link budget. For where the fast wiggles come from, see Multipath, delay spread and fading.

Everyday version. Walk between two radios playing the same station and at some point you turn one down and the other up. If you swap every time a passing lorry makes the second one briefly louder, you spend the whole walk swapping. If you refuse to swap until the first is inaudible, you miss part of the programme. The network faces exactly that choice, thousands of times a second, and the two knobs below are how it is settled.

    What the controls are teaching

    The handover margin Δ and the hysteresis H fail in opposite directions, and a real network is tuned between them. Push Δ up and the threshold Pmin + Δ sits high above the floor, so the trigger arms far from the boundary and fires again every time shadowing moves the two levels past one another: frequent, unnecessary handovers, each one a signalling transaction and a brief gap in the speech. Pull Δ down to zero and the trigger only arms when the link is already at the point of failure, leaving no time to complete the switch, which is the course's second failure mode and the one that shows up as dropped calls. H is the separate question of how much better the neighbour has to be before the switch is worth making: at zero it lets every shadow crossing start a handover, and the ping-pong counter climbs; too large and the mobile is dragged deep into the next cell on a dying link. The running average is what makes any of it workable, because it turns a trace that crosses the threshold dozens of times into one that crosses it once.

    Three system-level results sit behind the same picture. Cell splitting is visible on the base station power card: the transmitter is sized so the received level at the cell vertex hits Pmin plus a 6 dB margin, so halving R cuts the required power by 10 n log102, which is 12.0 dB at n = 4 — and buys four times the capacity per unit area, paid for with twice as many handovers along any route and twice as many base stations along it. Sectorisation cuts the first-tier interferers from six to two at 120 degrees, worth 10 log103 = 4.77 dB of SIR, and to one at 60 degrees; on its own it also fragments the channel pool and hurts trunking efficiency, so it pays only when it is combined with a smaller cluster. Closed-loop power control changes nothing about the geometry but takes several decibels off what every mobile radiates, which is why a CDMA system, where every user shares the band, cannot work without it.

    Honest limits of this model. Propagation is one log-distance slope, Pr = Pt + Gt + Gr − [PL(d0) + 10 n log10(d/d0)] + Xσ, with a free-space reference at d0 = 1 m and 900 MHz; the shadowing of the two cells is generated independently, where real terrain correlates them; the sector antennas are the course's ideal wedges seeing exactly 6, 2 or 1 first-tier interferer; the SIR card is the simplified variant with all interferers placed at the reuse distance for N = 7, so it reads several decibels above the practical and conservative variants; the fast fading pattern used in panel 3 repeats every 682 m; and the clock runs eight times faster than real time so that a drive across a cell takes seconds rather than minutes.

    Glossary
    Handover
    Moving a call in progress from one base station to another without dropping it. Called handoff in North American texts.
    Pmin
    The weakest received level at which the link still works. The course quotes −100 to −90 dBm.
    PHO
    The handover threshold, set above Pmin by the margin Δ. The trigger arms when the serving level falls to it.
    Handover margin Δ
    PHO − Pmin. Too large gives frequent unnecessary handovers; too small leaves insufficient time to complete one.
    Hysteresis H
    How many decibels stronger the neighbour must be before the switch is made. Its job is to stop ping-pong.
    Dwell timer
    How long the trigger condition must hold continuously before the handover is started.
    Ping-pong
    Handing back to the cell just left, within a few seconds. Pure overhead: signalling load and speech gaps for no coverage gain.
    Shadowing, Xσ
    Slow level variation from obstruction, log-normal with standard deviation σ in dB, correlated along a route over roughly 20 m.
    Path-loss exponent n
    How fast power falls with distance: 2 in free space, about 4 with a ground reflection, more when the path is obstructed.
    Running average
    Smoothing the level over a window of road before comparing it with a threshold, so momentary fades do not trigger a handover.
    Cell radius R
    Centre to vertex of the hexagon. Adjacent cell centres are √3 R apart, not 2R.
    Cell splitting
    Replacing a cell with smaller ones. Transmit power scales as Rn, capacity per unit area as 1/R², and the cluster size N does not change.
    Sectorisation
    Replacing the omni antenna with directional ones. At 120 degrees each sector sees 2 first-tier interferers instead of 6.
    SIR
    Signal to interference ratio. Here the simplified first-tier form, (√(3N))n divided by the number of interferers.
    Reuse distance D
    Centre-to-centre spacing of the nearest cells using the same channels, R√(3N). For N = 7 that is 4.583 R.
    Closed-loop power control
    The base station tells the mobile to raise or lower its power one step at a time, so the level arriving at the base station stays on target.
    Near-far problem
    A close mobile drowning a distant one. Severe in CDMA, where everyone shares the band, which is why its power control runs at 800 or 1500 Hz.
