Fresnel zones, earth bulge and path clearance

A radio beam is not a pencil line. The energy that reaches the far antenna travels through a fat cigar-shaped region wrapped around the straight path — the first Fresnel zone — and anything poking into that region steals signal even when you can still see the far antenna clearly. On top of that the earth itself bulges up in the middle of a long hop, by an amount that changes with the weather. This tool draws the hop to scale, computes how much of the zone is blocked, and turns that into decibels.

New to decibels and received power? Start with link budget, then come back. Everything here is a correction that gets subtracted inside that budget.

An everyday analogy. Shine a torch at a wall and slide a book up from below. The shadow does not appear the instant the book crosses the straight line from bulb to wall — the light dims gradually before that, and the edge of the shadow is soft, with faint bright and dark bands beside it. Radio does the same thing, on a scale of metres to hundreds of metres, and those bands are what this page is about.

Guided tour

1 · The path, drawn to scale — two masts, the bulging earth between them, and the Fresnel zone wrapped around the sight line

  • The blue cigar is the first Fresnel zone. Its half-width at any point is F₁, and it pinches to nothing at each mast. Keep it clear and the link behaves as if nothing were there.
  • The grey curve is the earth. Height is measured from the straight chord joining the two mast bases, so the ends sit at zero and the middle rises by the bulge.
  • The solid dark line is the line of sight from one mast top to the other. The dashed line below it is the 0.6 F₁ working limit — the obstacle must stay below that, not merely below the sight line.
  • The vertical arrow at the obstacle is the clearance: how far the sight line passes above the top of the obstacle, in metres and in units of F₁.
  • The horizontal scale is kilometres and the vertical scale is metres, so the picture is stretched vertically. That is how every real path profile is drawn.

2 · What the blockage costs — knife-edge diffraction loss J(ν) from the exact Fresnel integrals

  • The blue curve is the loss caused by a single sharp-topped obstacle, computed from the Fresnel cosine and sine integrals. Loss increases downwards, so lower means worse.
  • Left of zero the obstacle is above the sight line. Right of zero it is below, and the tool still charges you loss until the clearance reaches about 0.55 F₁.
  • Beyond that the curve rises above the free-space line and rings: the diffracted field alternately adds to and subtracts from the direct field, up to about 1.4 dB either way. That ripple is real, not noise in the plot.
  • The dashed orange curve is the closed-form approximation printed in ITU-R P.526. It tracks the exact answer beautifully to the right of its stated validity limit ν = −0.78, and diverges without warning past it. That is why the limit is stated.
  • The pink dot is your current path.

3 · Why more clearance is not always better — the ground-reflected ray, and the comb of nulls it makes over water

  • This panel assumes the direct path is open and asks a different question: a second ray bounces off the surface and arrives late. Its extra path length, measured in half-wavelengths, is exactly the Fresnel zone number n = (clearance / F₁)² at the bounce point.
  • Odd n means the two rays arrive in phase and the signal is up to 6 dB stronger than free space. Even n means they arrive out of phase and cancel.
  • Over smooth sea almost all the energy comes back, so the nulls are deep — tens of decibels. Over rough land most of it scatters away and the ripple is a couple of decibels.
  • Raising a mast slides you along this axis. On a sea path that can walk you straight into a null, which is why over-water hops are designed to sit on a peak, not merely high.
The arithmetic, worked through on a textbook example

Press guided-tour step 4 to put the tool into this state and read the numbers off the cards.

  1. A 1 km hop at 150 MHz, that is f = 0.15 GHz. Both masts are 27 m. A ridge 21 m high sits at mid-path, so d₁ = d₂ = 0.5 km.
  2. First Fresnel radius: F₁ = 17.3 √(0.5 × 0.5 / (0.15 × 1)) = 17.3 × 1.291 = 22.33 m.
  3. Earth bulge at k = 4/3: 0.5 × 0.5 / (12.75 × 1.333) = 0.01 m. Negligible on a 1 km hop, which is the point of the k control — it does nothing here and dominates at 100 km.
  4. The sight line runs level at 27 m, so the clearance is 27 − 21 − 0.01 = 5.99 m, which is 5.99 / 22.33 = 0.27 F₁.
  5. Diffraction parameter: ν = −√2 × 0.27 = −0.379.
  6. Exact Fresnel-integral loss J(−0.379) = 2.80 dB. The P.526 closed form gives 2.88 dB. The course notes read 2.7 dB off a printed graph. All three agree to about a tenth of a decibel, and the spread between them is the whole argument for computing rather than eyeballing.
  7. The criterion check: 0.27 F₁ is well under 0.6 F₁, so this path fails the normal microwave clearance rule even though you can see straight over the ridge.

Three habits come out of these three panels. First, clearance is counted in Fresnel radii, never in metres: the same ridge that is harmless on a short 6 GHz hop is fatal on a long 150 MHz one, because F₁ grows with path length and shrinks with frequency. Second, the atmosphere is part of the geometry — the k factor stretches or flattens the effective earth, and a path that clears comfortably on a median day at k = 4/3 can be blocked by its own planet on a sub-refractive night at k = 1/2, which is why a clearance figure is meaningless unless a worst-case k is quoted beside it. Third, clearance has to be constrained from both sides over a reflecting surface, because the reflected ray does not care that you have plenty of room. A planner picks mast heights that satisfy 0.6 F₁ at k = 2/3 and, on a sea or lake path, land on an odd Fresnel zone at the bounce point rather than an even one.

Where this model is simplified: the obstacle is treated as a single ideal knife edge, so a broad rounded hill or two ridges in a row will lose more than the figure shown; ITU-R P.526 gives rounded-obstacle and multiple-edge corrections for those. The reflection panel assumes one specular bounce off a plane surface, with the reflection magnitude fixed at 0.95 for sea and 0.30 for land rather than computed from grazing angle, polarisation and sea state. Atmospheric absorption, rain and antenna patterns are not included here at all.

Glossary
Fresnel zone
The region around the straight path within which a reflected or scattered ray arrives less than half a wavelength late. The first zone is the innermost one and carries most of the energy.
F₁
The radius of the first Fresnel zone at a given point along the path, in metres. Widest at mid-path, zero at each antenna.
Clearance
How far the straight sight line passes above the top of the obstacle. Negative clearance means the obstacle is above the sight line and you cannot see the far antenna.
Diffraction
The bending of a wave around an edge. It is why a blocked radio path still delivers some signal instead of none.
ν (nu)
The dimensionless diffraction parameter. ν = −√2 × clearance / F₁. Positive ν means blocked, negative means clear.
J(ν)
The knife-edge diffraction loss in decibels as a function of ν. J(0) = 6.02 dB, the loss when the obstacle just grazes the sight line.
dB, decibel
A ratio on a logarithmic scale. 3 dB is half the power, 10 dB is a tenth, 20 dB is a hundredth.
Earth bulge
The height by which the curved earth rises above the straight chord joining two points on it. About 147 m in the middle of a 100 km path at k = 4/3.
k factor
The factor by which the earth's radius appears multiplied because the atmosphere bends radio waves downward. The median value is 4/3; it falls toward 1/2 in sub-refractive conditions, which doubles the bulge.
Grazing
The obstacle top sitting exactly on the sight line, clearance zero.
Specular reflection
A mirror-like bounce off a smooth surface, at an equal angle. Sea and lakes do this to radio; rough ground scatters instead.
Reflection magnitude
The fraction of the field amplitude that survives the bounce. Near 1 for smooth water at a shallow angle, small for rough land.
