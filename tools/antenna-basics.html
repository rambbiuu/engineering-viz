Antenna patterns, gain and beamwidth

The map of where an antenna sends its energy is its radiation pattern. Gain, beamwidth, sidelobes and front-to-back ratio are four numbers read off that one map.

If decibels, dBi or boresight are new words, start with link budget, which introduces the decibel bookkeeping this tool feeds.

Guided tour

Step through the five buttons above and the sliders move for you. Step 1 is the half-wave dipole, the reference every wire antenna is compared against.

1 · The hardware, drawn to human scale — the metal itself on a mast, with the beam it makes coming out of it

  • shaded shape — where radiation goes
  • everything drawn to the same metre scale
  • dashed line — mechanical boresight, 0° right

2 · The pattern as a polar cut — the same beam seen from the side; rings are decibels below the peak

  • distance from the centre is power relative to the peak: the outer ring is the peak, the centre is 40 dB down (one ten-thousandth of the power)
  • the shaded wedge is the half-power beamwidth, the angular width over which at least half the peak power is delivered
  • ticks outside the circle mark nulls — directions in which this antenna radiates nothing at all

3 · The same cut on a decibel scale — unrolled into a straight axis, which is how sidelobes are actually read

  • the horizontal axis is direction in degrees, using the same zero as the pictures above; the window narrows automatically as the beam does
  • the upper dashed line is 3 dB below the peak; the width of the curve at that line is the half-power beamwidth
  • the lower dashed line sits on the tallest sidelobe — the leak that lands on somebody else's receiver

    Directivity here is a genuine numerical integral of the plotted field pattern, D = 2 / ∫0π |F(θ)|2 sin θ dθ, evaluated with Simpson's rule over 20 000 intervals. Nothing on this page is read from a table of typical antenna gains.

    Three trades live in the five buttons. First, concentration is the only way to get gain: a dipole spreads power over almost the whole sky and manages 2.15 dBi, while a 6λ dish squeezes it into ten degrees and reaches 25 dBi, and the only thing that changed was the shape of the map. Second, aperture in wavelengths is what counts — hold the dish at 6λ and slide the frequency from 0.3 to 30 GHz: the pattern never moves, only the hardware shrinks from three metres to three centimetres. Third, sidelobes are the price of a hard-edged aperture: a uniformly illuminated line or array always leaks at −13.3 dB no matter how large it is, a round aperture improves that to −17.6 dB for free, and buying anything better means tapering the illumination, which costs both gain and beamwidth.

    Words used here
    Wavelength λ
    The distance from one wave peak to the next, λ = c/f. At 2.4 GHz it is 12.5 cm. Antenna behaviour depends on size measured in wavelengths, never on size in metres alone.
    Radiation pattern
    How much power the antenna sends in each direction, drawn relative to its own strongest direction.
    Isotropic radiator
    An imaginary antenna that radiates equally in every direction. It cannot be built, but it is the yardstick for gain.
    Directivity D
    Peak power density divided by the average over all directions. A pure shape number: it says how tightly the energy is concentrated, ignoring any heat lost in the antenna.
    Radiation efficiency η
    The fraction of the power fed in that actually leaves as radio waves. The rest becomes heat in the conductor, the dielectric and the matching network.
    Gain G, in dBi
    G = ηD, quoted in decibels relative to the isotropic radiator. The "i" means isotropic. Gain is what a link budget uses, because it counts the losses.
    Half-power beamwidth, HPBW
    The angle across the main lobe between the two directions where the power has fallen to half, that is 3 dB below, the peak.
    Sidelobe
    Any lobe other than the main one. Quoted in decibels below the peak; it is the main cause of interference into neighbouring links.
    Null
    A direction of zero radiation between lobes. Useful: point a null at an interferer and it disappears.
    Boresight
    Mechanical boresight is the direction the structure is built to point. Electrical boresight is where the beam actually peaks. The angle between them is the boresight error, and calibrating it out is a real production step.
    Effective aperture Ae
    The area of the incoming wavefront the antenna effectively collects, Ae = Gλ2/4π. Even a wire has one.
    Array factor
    The pattern a set of point sources makes purely by interference of their feeds. The real pattern is the array factor multiplied by the pattern of one element.
