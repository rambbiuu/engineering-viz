Pulse shaping, ISI and the eye diagram

A real channel smears every pulse into its neighbours — intersymbol interference. Shape the pulse so it is zero at every other sampling instant, and the eye diagram shows the margin that remains.

1 · What the transmitter actually puts on the wire — the bits, the symbol levels they choose, and the shaped waveform that carries them, drawn to scale in milliseconds

  • The orange stems are the wanted symbol values — one per symbol period T, each carrying log2M bits.
  • The blue curve is the real transmitted voltage. It is smooth, it wanders between the stems, and it overshoots.
  • The blue dots sit at the sampling instants t = kT. With a raised cosine every dot lands exactly on its own stem, no matter what the neighbours are doing.

2 · The pulse that makes this work — one raised-cosine pulse, and its neighbours, sampled at the symbol instants

  • The blue curve is one pulse h(t), scaled so its peak is 1. The purple curves are the pulses one symbol earlier and one symbol later.
  • The vertical grey lines are the sampling instants, spaced one symbol period T apart.
  • The dots show what each pulse contributes at those instants.

3 · The eye diagram — every symbol period cut out and laid on top of the others, with noise added

  • Each faint blue line is two symbol periods of the received waveform, folded onto the same time axis. Hundreds of them overlap.
  • The green vertical line is where the receiver samples. Drag the timing offset to move it.
  • The green bracket is the eye height — the smallest vertical gap between the highest trace of one level and the lowest trace of the level above. Noise larger than half that gap causes a bit error.
  • The orange bracket is the eye width, the horizontal gap at the decision threshold. It is the timing error the link can absorb.

4 · What it costs in spectrum — the filter gain against frequency, and the band the signal actually occupies

  • The blue curve is the filter's gain |H(f)|. Outside the bracket it is exactly zero: nothing is transmitted there.
  • The green bracket is the occupied bandwidth once modulated onto a carrier, B = Rs(1 + β) (RF); at baseband it is half this. The dashed lines at ±Rs/2 mark the Nyquist minimum, the narrowest band that can carry Rs symbols per second without ISI.
  • The gap between the dashed lines and the bracket is the excess bandwidth, and it is exactly the fraction β of the minimum. That is the price of a usable eye.

    An everyday version of the same idea

    Think of a row of people passing buckets down a line, one bucket per second. If each person swings wildly, water from one bucket slops into the next and nobody can tell how full any single bucket was. Pulse shaping is agreeing on a swing that is guaranteed to be perfectly still at the exact moment the next bucket is read. The swing takes longer and needs more room — that extra room is the excess bandwidth β.

    Glossary
    Symbol
    One transmitted level, held for one symbol period T. A symbol carries log2M bits, so with four levels one symbol carries two bits.
    Baud (Bd)
    Symbols per second. The symbol rate Rs = 1/T. Two kilobaud means two thousand symbols every second, not two thousand bits.
    Unit interval (UI)
    Another name for one symbol period T. The eye diagram is built by cutting the waveform into unit intervals and stacking them.
    Intersymbol interference (ISI)
    Energy from one symbol's pulse still present when the next symbol is sampled. It shifts the sampled voltage away from the level that was sent, and it is what closes the eye.
    Nyquist criterion for zero ISI
    The rule a pulse must obey: the end-to-end response must be zero at every symbol instant except its own. The raised cosine is the standard family of shapes that obeys it.
    Raised cosine
    A pulse whose spectrum is flat in the middle and rolls off with a cosine skirt on each edge. Named for the shape of that skirt in frequency, not in time.
    Roll-off β
    How wide those cosine skirts are, from 0 to 1. It is also exactly the fraction of extra bandwidth used above the theoretical minimum, which is why it is also called the excess bandwidth factor.
    Root raised cosine (RRC)
    A filter whose gain is the square root of the raised cosine's. One in the transmitter and one in the receiver multiply to give the full raised cosine, so the pair is ISI-free even though neither half is on its own.
    Matched filter
    A receive filter shaped like the transmitted pulse. It maximises signal-to-noise ratio at the sampling instant, which is the reason for splitting the shaping in half rather than doing it all at the transmitter.
    Eye diagram
    Hundreds of unit intervals of the received signal drawn on top of one another. The clear space in the middle is the eye.
    Eye height
    The vertical opening at the sampling instant. It is the voltage headroom before noise causes a wrong decision.
    Eye width
    The horizontal opening at the decision threshold. It is how far the receiver's clock may drift before the decision goes wrong.
    Decision threshold
    The voltage halfway between two neighbouring symbol levels. The receiver decides which level was sent by comparing the sample against these.
    Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
    Mean signal power divided by mean noise power, in decibels. Here the noise power is the power inside the occupied bandwidth B; an SNR quoted without its reference bandwidth means nothing.
    Spectral efficiency
    Bits per second delivered for each hertz of bandwidth used, in bit/s/Hz. The single number a regulator and a system designer both care about.
    M-PAM
    M-level pulse amplitude modulation: the symbol is one of M evenly spaced voltages. This page uses M = 2, 4 or 8.
    Three trades live in these sliders

    Every digital radio makes all three. Roll-off β buys timing tolerance with bandwidth: drop it to zero for the narrowest possible signal and the pulse tails decay only as 1/t, so a fraction of a symbol period of clock error closes the eye completely; raise it to one and the eye stays open across almost the whole interval, for double the spectrum. Levels M buy data rate with noise margin: going from two levels to eight triples the bit rate at the same bandwidth, but the gaps between levels shrink to a seventh, which costs about 13 dB of noise margin at the same average-power SNR. And splitting the shaping into a root raised cosine at each end costs nothing in bandwidth while gaining the matched-filter SNR advantage — press the second filter button to see that the transmitted waveform on its own genuinely is not ISI-free, and that only the cascade is. Real links go further than this page does: they add a channel that is not flat, a clock recovered from the data rather than given, and an equaliser to undo whatever the channel did.
