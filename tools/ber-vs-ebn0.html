Bit error rate against Eb/N0

A receiver guesses each bit from one noisy voltage; the fraction it gets wrong is the bit error rate. Here it is plotted against Eb/N0, in clean noise and in fading.

Error-correcting coding lets the demodulator work at a lower Eb/N0 for the same delivered error rate, which is worth a fixed number of decibels. Implementation loss is the opposite: the decibels a real receiver wastes on imperfect timing, imperfect carrier phase and quantisation. Both are modelled here as a straight shift of the working point, and only the coding gain in decibels is modelled, not the extra bandwidth a code needs.

        The arithmetic, on the settings as they stand

          The shape of the curve matters more than its position. In additive noise the error rate falls off a cliff, so a link that is 2 dB short is nearly fixed by a slightly better amplifier. Under Rayleigh fading the same scheme gives a straight, shallow slope of one decade of error rate per decade of power, because the errors are dominated by the moments the signal happens to be deeply faded, and no amount of average power removes those moments. That is why chasing a fading link with transmit power is the wrong move — the required margin runs to tens of decibels — and why every mobile system instead buys independent copies of the signal. Watch the slope steepen from one to two to four as branches are added: four branches recover most of what fading took, at a fraction of the power a single branch would need. Three simplifications are worth naming. The fading is flat and slowly varying, and the branches are assumed perfectly independent and combined by maximal-ratio combining, which is the best case; the M-PSK and M-QAM curves use the standard nearest-neighbour approximation with Gray coding, which is accurate at the error rates that matter and slightly pessimistic at very low Eb/N0; and Eb/N0 here is the mean per branch, so L branches also collect L times the energy.

          Words used on this page
          Bit error rate (BER)
          The fraction of received bits that come out wrong. 1 × 10−6 means one bit in a million. Voice can live with 10−3; a file transfer wants 10−9 or better before coding.
          Eb/N0
          Energy received per information bit, divided by the noise power in one hertz of bandwidth. It is a ratio, quoted in dB. Unlike a plain signal-to-noise ratio it does not change when you change the bit rate or the bandwidth, so it is the fair way to compare two modulation schemes.
          Es/N0
          The same thing per transmitted symbol rather than per bit. A symbol carries log2M bits, so Es/N0 = (Eb/N0) × log2M.
          N0
          Noise power spectral density, in watts per hertz. At room temperature a perfect receiver sees −174 dBm in every hertz.
          Q function
          The area under the tail of a standard bell curve beyond x standard deviations: Q(x) = 0.5 erfc(x/√2). It is the probability that noise pushes a sample past the decision threshold.
          AWGN
          Additive white Gaussian noise. The plain, well-behaved channel: the signal arrives at constant strength and only thermal noise is added. Satellite and line-of-sight links come close to it.
          Rayleigh fading
          What a mobile radio channel looks like with no direct path. Many reflections arrive with random phases, so the received amplitude wanders and occasionally collapses. The received power follows an exponential distribution about its mean.
          Rician fading
          The same reflections, plus one dominant direct path. The K factor is the power in that direct path divided by the power in the scattered paths, in dB. Large K approaches AWGN; K approaching zero approaches Rayleigh.
          Flat fading
          Fading that affects the whole signal bandwidth equally, so it scales the amplitude without distorting the waveform. All the fading formulas here assume it.
          Diversity
          Receiving the same information over more than one independently fading path — two antennas, two frequencies, two time slots. Deep fades on every path at once are rare, so the combined signal fades far less.
          Maximal-ratio combining (MRC)
          The best way to add diversity branches: weight each by its own signal strength before summing. The combined signal-to-noise ratio is the sum of the branch ratios.
          Diversity order L
          The number of independently fading branches. At high Eb/N0 the error rate falls as the L-th power of the signal-to-noise ratio, so the curve on this plot steepens to L decades of error rate per decade of power.
          Gray coding
          Numbering the constellation points so neighbours differ in one bit only. A symbol error then usually costs one bit, which is why the bit error rate is roughly the symbol error rate divided by log2M.
          Coding gain
          The decibels of Eb/N0 an error-correcting code saves you at a stated output error rate.
          Implementation loss
          The decibels a real receiver falls short of theory because of timing error, phase error and quantisation. One to two dB is normal.
          Spectral efficiency
          Information bits carried per second in each hertz of bandwidth. Higher-order schemes buy it and pay for it in required Eb/N0.
