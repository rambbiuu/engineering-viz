HF, VHF and how radio waves travel

Below 30 MHz the sky is a mirror: the ionosphere — layers of charged air 60–400 km up — bends radio waves back to Earth, letting HF cross oceans with no infrastructure. Above 30 MHz the mirror turns to glass and waves fly straight through, so VHF and up live within line of sight. Slide the frequency across the boundary and watch the sky change its mind.

Things to try: park an MF frequency (1 MHz, AM radio) and flip day to night — the absorbing D layer evaporates and suddenly the sky wave bounces, which is why distant AM stations appear after sunset. Walk HF upward until the rays start punching through: steep rays escape first, shallow ones hold on longest, and past the maximum usable frequency the sky goes silent. Then jump to VHF and note the only thing left is the line-of-sight ray — FM, aircraft, and your 2–18 GHz geolocation work all live in this straight-line world. Heights are real kilometres; the physics is simplified (flat Earth, single-layer secant law) but the behaviour boundaries are honest.
