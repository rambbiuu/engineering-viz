Noise figure, cascades and receiver sensitivity

Every receiver adds noise of its own, and that self-made noise sets the weakest signal it can still hear. This tool builds a receive chain one block at a time — cable, amplifier, filter, mixer — and works out how much noise the whole chain adds, where that noise comes from, and what the resulting sensitivity is in dBm. The order of the blocks matters enormously, which is the single most useful thing to take away.

New to decibels and to dBm? Start with the link budget tool, which explains dBm and the noise floor from scratch.

A cascade of stages behaves like a chain of photocopies. Whatever specks the first copy adds are enlarged again by every machine after it, so a fleck on the first sheet ends up the size of a coin. Grit added by the last machine is copied no further and barely shows. Noise in a receiver works the same way: the first block's noise is amplified by everything downstream, so it dominates.

Guided tour

Step 1 sets the chain back to its starting state: a 15 dB run of coaxial feeder cable between the antenna and the amplifier. Every decibel of that cable loss lands directly on the system noise figure.

    The arithmetic, on the chain as it stands

      Words used on this page

      dB (decibel)
      A ratio written on a logarithmic scale: 10 log10 of a power ratio. +3 dB is roughly double the power, −3 dB is roughly half, +10 dB is ten times.
      dBm
      An absolute power, measured in dB relative to one milliwatt. 0 dBm is 1 mW; −100 dBm is 0.1 picowatt, a normal size for a received radio signal.
      Thermal noise
      The random voltage every warm resistor produces, purely because its electrons jiggle. Its power is k T B watts, where k is Boltzmann's constant, T the temperature in kelvin and B the bandwidth in hertz. At room temperature that comes to −174 dBm in every hertz of bandwidth.
      Noise bandwidth B
      The width in hertz of the filter that decides how much of that noise the receiver lets through. Doubling it lets in twice the noise, which is 3.01 dB more.
      Noise factor F
      The number of times a block worsens the signal-to-noise ratio: F = SNRin / SNRout, with both ratios as plain numbers, not dB. F is never less than 1.
      Noise figure NF
      The same thing written in dB: NF = 10 log10 F. NF = 3 dB means the block adds as much noise as it received, so it throws away half the signal-to-noise ratio.
      Noise temperature Te
      An equivalent way to state the same noise: the temperature a perfect, noiseless block's source would have to be at to make that much noise. Te = (F − 1) × 290 K.
      Passive block
      A block with no power supply, such as cable, a filter or a diode mixer. It can only attenuate, and its noise figure in dB equals its loss in dB.
      Insertion loss, conversion loss
      The signal a passive block swallows, in dB. For a mixer it is called conversion loss because the signal also changes frequency on the way through.
      LNA (low-noise amplifier)
      The first amplifier in a receiver, chosen for the lowest noise figure that can be bought rather than the most gain. Its job is to lift the signal above the noise of everything behind it.
      Friis cascade formula
      The rule that adds up a chain's noise: F = F1 + (F2−1)/G1 + (F3−1)/(G1G2) + … Every term must be a plain ratio, never dB, and the answer is converted to dB only at the very end.
      Noise floor
      The receiver's own noise power in the wanted bandwidth, in dBm. Any signal below it is buried.
      SNR (signal-to-noise ratio)
      How far the wanted signal sits above the noise, in dB. Each modulation scheme needs some minimum SNR to work at its target error rate.
      Sensitivity
      The weakest signal, in dBm, that still arrives with the required SNR. Noise floor plus required SNR.
      OIP3 and IIP3
      Output and input third-order intercept points, in dBm. They measure how well a block copes with strong signals before it starts generating spurious tones. IIP3 = OIP3 − gain.
      SFDR (spurious-free dynamic range)
      The span in dB between the weakest signal a receiver can hear and the strongest one it can hear without its own intermodulation products rising above the noise floor.

      Two opposite rules govern the same chain. Noise is dominated by the front: every decibel of loss ahead of the first amplifier adds a decibel to the system noise figure, one for one, which is why moving the feeder cable from in front of the amplifier to behind it can shift sensitivity by more than 10 dB in a single click, and why real base stations bolt the amplifier to the mast beside the antenna instead of at the bottom of the tower. Large-signal behaviour is dominated by the back: the cascade intercept adds reciprocals weighted by the gain that follows, so it is the last, highest-level stage that saturates first. Design the front for noise, the back for headroom, and accept that gain early helps one and hurts the other. Two simplifications are worth naming: passive blocks are assumed to sit at the 290 K reference temperature, and the intercept sum assumes the worst case where every stage's distortion adds in phase.
