Every handset in a code-division system uses the whole band at once, separated only by its code. The correlator digs one signal back out — until a nearer handset shouts over it.

      Model, and where it is simplified
      1. An everyday picture: everyone talks at once in different languages, and you follow yours by language, not volume — until someone beside you shouts.
      2. Every doubling of the code length adds 3 dB of processing gain and 3 dB to the ratio the correlator delivers.
      3. Processing gain is a ratio, not a level: 24 dB of gain cannot rescue a signal 60 dB down.
      4. Power control is the real closed loop — one bit up or down per command, over a 3 s run against a Clarke sum-of-sinusoids Rayleigh channel — and the residual error is measured from that simulation. The fading has one tap and no shadowing, and the loop has no measurement error or feedback delay, so the residual is optimistic.
      5. Walsh rows are treated as perfectly chip-aligned. That holds on the downlink from one base station and fails on the uplink drawn in section 1, which is why the other users vanish in Walsh mode.
      6. PN interference is measured from the actual cross-correlations of the generated m-sequence over 64 bits, so it lands within about a decibel of the textbook Gp.
      7. The capacity card assumes perfect power control and equal rates for everyone.
      Glossary
      Chip
      One symbol of the spreading code. Chips are much shorter than data bits: Tc against Tb.
      Spreading
      Multiplying the data by the code, s(t) = d(t)c(t). It widens the bandwidth from the data rate to the chip rate without adding power.
      Despreading
      Multiplying the received signal by the same code again and averaging over one bit. The wanted signal collapses back to narrowband; everything else stays spread.
      Processing gain Gp
      The bandwidth expansion ratio, Rc/Rb = Tb/Tc. In decibels it is 10·log10 of that ratio, and it is how many decibels the correlator adds to the signal-to-interference ratio.
      Correlator
      The circuit that computes z = (1/Tb)∫r(t)c(t)dt over one bit period. Its output is the despread symbol.
      Cross-correlation
      The average product of two codes, chip by chip. Zero means the two signals do not disturb each other at all.
      Walsh–Hadamard code
      Rows of a matrix built by H2n = [[Hn, Hn], [Hn, −Hn]]. Any two distinct rows have exactly zero cross-correlation, provided they arrive perfectly aligned.
      PN m-sequence
      A pseudo-noise sequence from a shift register with feedback, of period 2m−1. Its autocorrelation is 1 at zero lag and exactly −1/L at every other lag, which is what makes it look like noise.
      Near-far problem
      A handset close to the base station arrives far stronger than a distant one. Because both occupy the same band, the strong one drowns the weak one no matter what codes they use.
      Power control
      The base station tells each handset to raise or lower its power, one bit per command and one step per bit. GSM does this at 2 Hz, IS-95 at 800 Hz, WCDMA at 1500 Hz.
      SIR
      Signal-to-interference ratio: wanted power divided by everything else. Quoted before despreading it is measured at chip level and is usually negative; after despreading it is the ratio the decision circuit sees.
      Eb/N0
      Energy per data bit divided by noise-plus-interference power density. It is the same number as the SIR after despreading, and it is what a modem specification quotes.
      Pole capacity
      The user count at which interference alone consumes the whole link budget: N = 1 + (W/R)/(Eb/N0)req. No amount of transmit power gets past it.
      Rayleigh fading
      The rapid level swings caused by reflections adding and cancelling as the handset moves. Deep fades come and go in milliseconds.
      Decibel (dB)
      A ratio on a logarithmic scale: 10·log10 of a power ratio. Ten decibels is ten times the power, 3 dB is roughly double.
