Time difference of arrival

Several receiver sites timestamp the same signal, and it reaches the nearest one first. Each pair of sites turns its arrival-time difference into a curve of possible positions, and the curves cross where the emitter is.

Worked example — the arithmetic on the numbers now on screen

    A nanosecond of clock error is 30 cm of range error, so a microsecond is 300 m — and the site geometry multiplies that by the PDOP before it reaches the answer. Drag the emitter outside the ring of sites and watch PDOP, and the expected miss with it, climb several times over for the same clocks.

    The engineering lesson, and where this model simplifies

    TDoA buys freedom from the antenna array that AoA needs, and pays for it with a clock-synchronisation problem. The exchange rate is fixed by the speed of light and cannot be argued with: 0.2998 m per nanosecond of error between any two sites. What an engineer can change is the geometry, and that is what PDOP measures — keep the emitter inside the ring of sites and PDOP sits near 1, let it drift outside and PDOP climbs past 5, multiplying every nanosecond by five before it lands on the map. Three sites in a plane give exactly two independent curves for two unknowns, so the residual is always zero and a broken clock is undetectable; the fourth site is the one that lets the system notice it is being lied to.

    Simplifications made here, stated plainly. The map is a flat 10 × 6 km plane, not the curved earth. Every path is direct line of sight, with no multipath and no atmospheric delay — both of which add a positive, correlated bias that this Gaussian model does not capture. Clock errors are drawn independently per site from a normal distribution, so the error on any pair's difference has rms √2 times the per-site figure. The sweep in section 3 solves each trial from a start near the true position, so it measures local sensitivity and not the chance of the solver landing somewhere else entirely.

    Glossary
    Time difference of arrival (TDoA)
    Finding an emitter from the differences between the times its signal reaches several receivers. No receiver needs to know when the signal was sent.
    Hyperbola
    The curve of all points whose distances to two fixed points differ by a fixed amount. One time difference gives one hyperbola; the emitter is somewhere on it. A difference of exactly zero flattens the curve into a straight line halfway between the two sites.
    Reference site
    The site every other site's timestamp is subtracted from. Which one you pick changes the curves drawn, but not the fix.
    Clock error
    How far a site's timestamp is from true time. Only the differences between sites matter, so a fault shared by all of them cancels out.
    rms
    Root mean square: the typical size of a fluctuating quantity. An rms clock error of 50 ns means most draws land within about 50 ns of zero.
    Sigma (σ)
    One standard deviation, the same thing as the rms figure here. About two thirds of draws fall inside ±1σ.
    Least squares
    Choosing the position that makes the leftover disagreement as small as possible when there are more measurements than unknowns.
    Residual
    What is left over after the best fit: how far the measurements still disagree with each other. Zero means there was no redundancy to check with.
    PDOP
    Position dilution of precision. Multiply the range error by PDOP to get the position error. It depends only on where the sites sit relative to the emitter, and it is evaluated here at the computed fix.
    c
    The speed of light, 299 792 458 m/s. Handily, that is 0.2998 m per nanosecond and 299.79 m per microsecond.
