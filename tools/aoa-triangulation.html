Angle-of-arrival triangulation

One site measures a direction, not a position. Cross several directions and you get a position — this tool draws one noisy measurement, its least-squares fix, and the error ellipse around it.

If measuring a bearing from phase is new, start with Phase interferometry. Next: Time difference of arrival, which solves the same problem with clocks instead of angles.

Everyday version. Three people stand apart in a field and each points at a distant church tower. Nobody points exactly right, so the three arms do not meet at a point — they enclose a small triangle. The fix is the best guess inside that triangle, and the triangle is the error.

    What the controls are teaching

    Two sites are a trap. Two lines in a plane always cross somewhere, so the residual is exactly zero and the fit looks perfect, yet the crossing sits wherever the two errors happened to push it and nothing on the screen warns you. The third bearing is the first one that can disagree, which is why it is the first one that gives you a residual to check the other two against. After that each mast buys less: the ellipse shrinks roughly as one over the square root of the number of sites, while moving one mast to fix a shallow crossing angle can halve it in a single drag.

    Watch what geometry does on its own. Drag two masts close together and their bearings become nearly parallel; the ellipse stretches along the line of sight until the fix is useless in range while still being sharp in cross-range, and the GDOP card climbs to say so. Push the emitter to the top of the map and every wedge widens in proportion, because an angle error is a distance error multiplied by range — that is the straight line in section 4, and it is the reason a network of cheap nearby sites beats one very accurate distant one.

    Four honest limits. The bearing errors here are independent, zero-mean and Gaussian, which real geolocation receivers are not — multipath puts a bias on a bearing, and a bias does not average away with more sites. The ellipse is a linearised covariance, exact only while the angle errors are small enough that the bearing lines are locally straight in the fit; at 10° rms it is indicative rather than precise. CEP = 0.59(σmaj + σmin) is the standard approximation, good to about one per cent while σmin/σmaj stays above 0.25 and optimistic below that. And the fit treats each bearing as a full line rather than a ray, so residuals are reported modulo 180°; where the geometry collapses — a crossing angle under about five degrees, or a fix landing on a site — the tool reports no fix instead of a confident number it cannot support.

    Glossary
    Angle of arrival
    The direction a signal came from, as measured at one receiving site. It fixes a line, not a point.
    Bearing
    That direction expressed as an angle clockwise from north, 000° to 360°.
    Triangulation
    Finding a position by crossing bearing lines from two or more known sites.
    Realisation
    One actual set of measurements drawn from the error distribution. Press "New measurement" for another.
    Least-squares fix
    The position that minimises the summed squared miss against every measured bearing line at once.
    Normal equations
    The small linear system M x = b whose solution is that minimum. Here M is 2 by 2, so it inverts by hand.
    Covariance matrix
    M⁻¹. Its diagonal holds the variances in east and north, its off-diagonal the tilt.
    Error ellipse
    The one-sigma contour of that covariance. About 39 % of fixes land inside a 2-D one-sigma ellipse.
    CEP
    Circular error probable: the radius of the circle containing half the fixes, here 0.59(σmaj + σmin).
    GDOP
    Geometric dilution of precision. How much the site layout alone multiplies measurement error into position error.
    Residual
    What is left over after the fit: the angle between each measured bearing and the bearing from that site to the fitted position, taken modulo 180° because the fit uses lines rather than rays.
    Cross-range error
    The sideways miss a bearing error causes at range R, about R·tanσ.
    Baseline
    The separation between two sites. A longer baseline gives a wider crossing angle and a rounder ellipse.
