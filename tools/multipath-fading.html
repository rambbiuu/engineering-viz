Multipath, delay spread and fading

Bounced copies of the same signal add or cancel at the receiver, so the level can collapse by 30 dB while the transmitter never changes. The tool shows one channel three ways: in delay, in frequency and in time.

If the idea of adding waves by phase is new, start with Fresnel zones and clearance. If decibels and margins are new, start with Link budget.

Everyday version. Shout across a canyon and you hear yourself back a moment later. If the echo arrives while you are still speaking, the two sounds overlap and some syllables turn to mush. A radio signal does the same thing, except the echoes arrive microseconds late instead of seconds, and instead of muddying words they cancel parts of the signal outright.

Coherence bandwidth definition
Move the reflectors — per-path delay and amplitude

Delay is how much later than the direct path a copy arrives; 1 microsecond of delay means 300 metres of extra travel. Amplitude is the size of that copy as a fraction of the strongest reflection. The direct path is not listed here — its strength is set by the K factor above.

1 · The street — one direct path plus reflections, each labelled with the extra distance it travels

  • The straight blue line is the direct path from the base station to the car. It is the shortest route, so its copy arrives first.
  • Each coloured triangle is a reflection off a wall. The label gives the extra distance and the delay that follows from it, at 300 metres per microsecond.
  • The reflection point sits halfway along, which is where the angle in equals the angle out. A copy that is late must have come off something far away.

2 · Power delay profile — how much power arrives at each delay, with the mean and the spread marked

  • Each vertical stem is one arriving copy, at its delay, at its power. Together they are the channel's fingerprint.
  • The dashed line is the mean excess delay τ̄ — the power-weighted average arrival time.
  • The shaded band is one RMS delay spread στ either side of it. That single number, not the number of stems, is what decides everything below.

3 · The channel across frequency — |H(f)| over the signal band; shaded columns are the notches

  • The blue curve is the channel gain at each frequency. Where copies arrive in step it is high; where they cancel it drops into a notch.
  • The pale vertical block is the signal's own bandwidth. If it fits inside one smooth hump the whole signal fades together — flat fading. If it straddles several notches, parts of the signal are wiped out while other parts are fine — frequency-selective fading.
  • The red columns mark where the gain is more than 10 dB below the average. Watch them slide sideways as the car drives.

4 · Received envelope as the car drives — signal strength against time, with the threshold and the fades marked

  • The blue trace is how strong the signal is, in dB relative to its own long-term RMS level. Time runs left to right and "now" is the right-hand edge.
  • The dashed line is the fade margin. Below it the link stops working; those stretches are shaded and are what an outage actually looks like.
  • The box on the left shows why. Each short coloured arrow is one path, drawn tip to tail; the long arrow is their sum. When the arrows curl back on themselves the sum shrinks into the small circle and the signal drops out.

    What the sliders are teaching

    Delay spread and Doppler are two independent axes of the same channel, and they hurt in different ways. Delay spread is about distance: it sets the coherence bandwidth, and once the signal is wider than that, no amount of transmit power helps — the notch is inside the signal, and the fix is an equaliser, a rake receiver, or splitting the signal into narrow subcarriers as OFDM does. Doppler is about speed: it sets how fast the pattern moves past the car, so a 30 dB fade at walking pace lasts long enough to lose a whole packet, while the same fade at 200 km/h is over in a millisecond and interleaving plus coding rides straight through it. Push the K factor to +20 dB and both problems shrink at once, because a dominant direct path leaves the reflections too weak to cancel it — which is exactly why a line of sight is worth so much more than the link budget alone suggests.

    Two honest limits of this model. The envelope drawn in section 4 is the exact vector sum of the handful of paths you set, so its statistics only approach the Rayleigh distribution as you add taps — with two paths it is a deterministic interference pattern, not a random one. And the outage, crossing rate and fade duration cards use the classical Rayleigh formulas, which assume many equal scatterers arriving from all directions; they are the numbers an engineer designs to, and the footnote on the outage card shows what the drawn waveform actually did so you can see the two disagree when there are too few paths.

    Glossary
    Multipath
    The signal reaching the receiver by several routes at once, having reflected off buildings, ground or hills.
    Tap
    One arriving copy in the model, described by its delay, its amplitude and its phase.
    Mean excess delay, τ̄
    The power-weighted average of the arrival delays, measured from the first copy.
    RMS delay spread, στ
    The power-weighted standard deviation of the arrival delays. One number summarising how smeared in time the channel is.
    Coherence bandwidth, Bc
    The width in frequency over which the channel gain stays roughly constant. Roughly 1/(5στ) if you want 0.5 correlation, 1/(50στ) if you want 0.9.
    Flat fading
    The signal is narrower than the coherence bandwidth, so every part of it fades by the same amount at the same moment.
    Frequency-selective fading
    The signal is wider than the coherence bandwidth, so some frequencies inside it are destroyed while others survive. This is what causes inter-symbol interference.
    Doppler shift, fd
    The frequency shift a moving receiver sees on one arriving path, (v/λ)cosθ, where θ is the angle between the path and the direction of travel.
    Maximum Doppler, fm
    v/λ, the shift on a path arriving from straight ahead. It sets how fast the fading pattern changes.
    Coherence time, Tc
    How long the channel stays roughly the same, about 0.423/fm.
    Rayleigh fading
    The envelope statistics when many similar scattered copies arrive and none dominates. Deep fades are common.
    Rician K factor
    The power in the dominant direct path divided by the power in everything scattered, A²/2σ², usually quoted in dB. Large K means a strong line of sight and shallow fades.
    Fade margin
    How many decibels of headroom the link is designed with, above the level at which it stops working.
    Outage
    The fraction of time the received level sits below that threshold.
    Level crossing rate
    How many times per second the envelope crosses the threshold going downwards.
    Average fade duration
    The mean length of one of those dropouts.
