AM, DSBSC, SSB and the cost of the carrier

A transmitter has a fixed number of watts, and in full AM most of them feed a carrier tone that carries no information. Four analogue schemes side by side, with a running account of where the power goes.

If waveforms and spectra are new, start with the modulation playground first. Every frequency here is a real number in hertz, and the carrier amplitude is fixed at 1 volt into 1 ohm so the power figures are watts.

            An everyday analogy, and the envelope detector's time constant

            Think of shouting a message across a valley. The carrier is the raw loudness of your voice; the message is the way you shape it. Full AM keeps shouting at full volume even during the silences, because the receiver on the far side finds the message by tracking how loud you are, and it needs a steady reference to compare against. Suppressed-carrier schemes stop shouting during the silences, which saves a great deal of breath, but then the far side has to guess the reference for itself — and if it guesses the timing wrong, the message comes back wrong.

            —

            Three trades live in these controls. Full AM buys a receiver that is a diode, a capacitor and a resistor, and pays for it with the carrier: at the legal limit of m = 1 only 33.3 per cent of the transmitter is doing work, and at the more typical m = 0.6 it is 15.3 per cent. Suppressing the carrier recovers all of that, but the receiver now needs a local oscillator locked to the transmitter, and the phase-error slider shows the penalty — output falls as cos φ and vanishes completely at 90 degrees, which is why practical receivers carry a Costas loop or a pilot tone. Single sideband then halves the bandwidth as well, and is remarkably tolerant of phase error because the error only rotates the recovered tone rather than shrinking it; what single sideband cannot tolerate is a frequency error, which shifts every component of the message by the same number of hertz and makes speech sound wrong even when nothing is lost. What is simplified here: the filters are ideal, there is no noise, the message is one or two pure tones so its Hilbert transform is exact, and the VSB filter is an idealised linear ramp — the course names VSB only in the AM taxonomy (analogue TV); the detailed filter shape lives in the textbooks (Haykin).

            Where to go next: the I/Q constellation takes the quadrature idea into digital modulation, and the superheterodyne receiver shows what the mixer in these detectors does to unwanted frequencies.

            Glossary
            Carrier
            The steady high-frequency sinusoid at fc that the message is loaded onto. On its own it carries no information.
            Sideband
            The band of frequencies either side of the carrier that appears when the message is applied. This is where the information actually lives.
            Modulation index m
            The ratio Am/Ac of message amplitude to carrier amplitude. It sets how deeply the carrier is varied.
            Envelope
            The slowly varying outline that the peaks of the modulated waveform trace out.
            Envelope detector
            A diode, capacitor and resistor that follow the envelope. It needs no local oscillator, which is why AM broadcast receivers are cheap.
            Overmodulation
            m greater than 1. The envelope folds through zero, the envelope detector output no longer matches the message, and the spectrum spreads.
            DSBSC
            Double-sideband suppressed carrier. The carrier line is removed and only the two sidebands are transmitted.
            Coherent detector
            A detector that multiplies the incoming signal by a locally generated copy of the carrier and then low-pass filters. It needs that copy to be at the right frequency and phase.
            Local oscillator
            The receiver's own copy of the carrier, used by a coherent detector.
            Phase error φ
            The angle by which the local oscillator leads or lags the true carrier.
            Hilbert transform
            An operation that shifts every frequency component of a signal by minus 90 degrees without changing its amplitude. It turns each cosine into a sine, and is how one sideband is cancelled.
            SSB
            Single sideband. One of the two sidebands is removed, halving the bandwidth and the power.
            VSB
            Vestigial sideband. One full sideband plus a small remnant of the other, so a realisable filter can do the job; analogue TV used it.
            Quadrature
            Two carriers at the same frequency but 90 degrees apart. They do not interfere, so two messages can share one band.
            QAM
            Quadrature amplitude modulation. Two DSBSC signals on quadrature carriers.
            Crosstalk
            Signal from one channel leaking into another. In QAM a phase error in the local oscillator produces crosstalk proportional to sin φ.
            FDM
            Frequency division multiplexing. Several messages stacked on different carriers so they occupy different parts of one wide band.
            Guard band
            Empty spectrum left between adjacent FDM channels so that a real, non-ideal filter can separate them.
            Bandwidth
            The range of positive frequency a signal occupies, in hertz.
