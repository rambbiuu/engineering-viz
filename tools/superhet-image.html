The superheterodyne receiver and its image

Nearly every radio built since 1920 slides the signal it wants down to one fixed frequency — the intermediate frequency, or IF — before doing any real work on it, because sharp filters and quiet amplifiers are far cheaper to build at one frequency than across a whole band. The device that does the sliding is a mixer, and a mixer can only measure the size of a frequency gap, never its direction. So a second, unwanted frequency slides down onto exactly the same IF and becomes inseparable from the wanted one. That frequency is the image, and stopping it before the mixer is the entire job of the filter at the front of the radio.

Everyday analogy: two people stand 3 m either side of you and shout. If all you can measure is that a voice came from 3 m away, you cannot say which one spoke — and once both voices reach your ears together, no amount of listening harder will separate them. You have to turn one of them away before they arrive. That is what the front-end filter does.

If decibels and dBm are unfamiliar, start with link budget, which builds them up from scratch. Everything below is in MHz for frequency and dBm for power.

Order is how many resonators the front-end filter contains. Each extra one steepens the skirt by 20 dB per decade of offset and costs money, size and insertion loss. OIP3 is the output third-order intercept point: the imaginary output level at which the mixer's third-order distortion would equal the wanted signal. Higher means a more linear, hungrier mixer.

1 · The receiver, block by block — what the hardware is, and what the signal looks like at three points along the chain

  • The green line is the wanted signal. The orange line is a strong neighbour sitting on the image frequency.
  • The three small pictures are spectra — a plot of how much power sits at each frequency. Height is power in dB, so a shorter line means a weaker signal.
  • Between the first and second picture the front-end filter has knocked the orange line down. Between the second and third the mixer has moved both of them onto the same IF, where they overlap exactly.
  • The local oscillator (LO) is the receiver's own internal tone. Tuning the radio means moving the LO, not the filters.

2 · What arrives at the antenna — the wanted signal, the image 2 × IF away, and how far the front-end filter pushes the image down

  • The blue curve is the front-end filter response, drawn as the level a neighbour of the chosen strength would still have after passing through it. It is flat at the top across the filter's passband and falls away either side.
  • The faint orange line is the image signal as it arrives at the antenna; the solid orange line is what is left of it after the filter. The vertical gap between them, marked on the plot, is the image rejection.
  • The green line is the wanted signal, held at a fixed −70 dBm so the comparison stays honest while you move the sliders.
  • The bar along the bottom measures 2 × IF, the gap between wanted and image. Raise the IF and the image walks further out into the filter's skirt.

3 · What comes out of the mixer — both signals now sit on the same intermediate frequency and no later filter can tell them apart

  • Both lines are drawn at the same frequency, the IF. They are nudged a few pixels apart only so you can see both; in reality they lie exactly on top of one another.
  • The number between them is the margin the receiver has left: how far below the wanted signal the image landed. Positive is good.
  • Nothing after the mixer can improve this number. Whatever margin the front-end filter bought, the rest of the radio inherits.

Worked example, following the controls above
    When two strong signals make a third

    The image is a frequency problem, and a filter fixes it. Intermodulation is a level problem, and no filter can fix it. Push a mixer or an amplifier hard enough and it stops being linear: two tones going in produce sums and differences at every combination n·f₁ ± m·f₂, and the order of each product is n + m. Even orders land near DC or near twice the carrier and filter out easily. The odd orders do not: third order, 2f₁−f₂ and 2f₂−f₁, falls one channel spacing outside the pair, inside the very passband you were protecting. The law that matters is that the third-order product rises 3 dB for every 1 dB the drive rises, so the clear space between wanted signal and rubbish closes at 2 dB per dB. Drag the nearby-signal slider and watch the two lines converge.

    4 · The 3:1 slope and the intercept — fundamental at slope 1, third order at slope 3, extrapolated to the point where they would meet

    • Green is the wanted output: 1 dB in, 1 dB out. Orange is the third-order product: 1 dB in, 3 dB out. Amber dashed is fifth order, at slope 5.
    • The circle where green and orange cross is OIP3, the output third-order intercept point. No real device reaches it — it is a straight-line extrapolation, which is exactly why it is useful as a single number.
    • The vertical line is the drive level you have set. The bracket beside it is the spurious-free window: how far the third-order product sits below the wanted signal right now.
    • The shaded band on the right is where a real device compresses, roughly 10 dB below OIP3. Past that line the straight lines stop being true and the tool is only extrapolating.

    Three trades live in these sliders. Raising the IF pushes the image further out into the filter's skirt and buys rejection almost for free — but it also makes the IF filter's fractional bandwidth smaller for the same channel, so selectivity gets harder; that is why 10.7 MHz suits an FM broadcast band 20 MHz wide, and why the same choice is hopeless at 2.4 GHz. Narrowing the front-end filter or adding poles buys rejection too, but a tracking filter that narrow has to stay tuned to the signal as you turn the dial, which is mechanically expensive. And when the fRF/IF ratio climbs past roughly 10 to 20, no single conversion works at all: the answer is two of them, a high first IF to kill the image and a low second IF to do the selecting. Simplifications to be aware of: the front-end response is an ideal Butterworth with no insertion loss and no stopband floor beyond a stated 100 dB cap, the mixer is treated as having 0 dB conversion gain so its input and output intercepts coincide, the fifth-order intercept is assumed to sit 5 dB below OIP3 because no general relation exists, and the wanted signal is pinned at −70 dBm.

    Glossary
    Superheterodyne (superhet)
    A receiver that shifts every incoming channel to one fixed frequency before filtering and detecting it, instead of building a tunable filter for each channel.
    Mixer
    A deliberately non-linear device fed with the incoming signal and the local oscillator. Its output contains the sum and difference of their frequencies.
    Local oscillator (LO)
    The tone the receiver generates itself. Tuning the radio means changing fLO.
    Intermediate frequency (IF)
    The fixed frequency everything is shifted to: fIF = |fRF − fLO|. Common values are 0.455, 10.7 and 70 MHz.
    Image frequency
    The second input frequency that produces the same IF, sitting 2 × IF away from the wanted signal on the other side of the LO.
    High-side / low-side injection
    Whether the LO is placed above the wanted signal (image above) or below it (image below).
    Image rejection
    The extra attenuation the front end applies at the image frequency compared with the wanted frequency, in dB. 60 dB means an equally strong image arrives 60 dB weaker.
    Preselector / front-end filter
    The bandpass filter between antenna and mixer. The only place the image can be removed.
    Butterworth order
    How many poles the filter has. |H(f)|² = 1/(1 + off2N): flat in the passband, and 20N dB steeper per decade of offset.
    dB and dBm
    dB is a ratio on a logarithmic scale: 10 dB is ten times the power, 3 dB is roughly double. dBm is an absolute power referred to one milliwatt, so 0 dBm = 1 mW and −70 dBm = 0.1 pW.
    Intermodulation (IM)
    New frequencies created when two or more signals pass through something non-linear together.
    Third-order product (IM3)
    The pair 2f₁−f₂ and 2f₂−f₁. It lands next to the original tones and rises 3 dB per 1 dB of drive.
    OIP3
    Output third-order intercept point, in dBm: the extrapolated output level where the fundamental and third-order lines cross.
    Spurious-free window
    How far the third-order product sits below the wanted output, in dB. Equal to 2 × (OIP3 − drive level).
    1 dB compression point
    The output level at which real gain has sagged 1 dB below the straight line. A rule of thumb puts it about 10 dB below OIP3.
