Frequency reuse, cluster size and SIR

A network has far fewer channels than users, so it spends the same frequencies again and again in different places. How far apart two cells must sit before they may share a channel is the whole design.

Glossary
Cell radius R
Centre of the cell to a corner of the hexagon, not to an edge. Neighbouring cell centres sit √3 R apart.
Cluster size N
How many cells share out the whole channel list before the pattern repeats. Only N = i² + ij + j² works: 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 12, 13… never 2, 5, 6.
Co-channel cells
Cells using the same channels. They interfere with each other and nothing can be done about it except distance or antenna direction.
Reuse distance D
Centre-to-centre distance to the nearest co-channel cell, D = R√3N.
Path loss exponent n
How fast power falls with distance: power ∝ d−n. n = 2 in free space, n ≈ 4 in a town.
SIR
Signal-to-interference ratio: wanted power divided by the sum of the co-channel powers. Noise is ignored; interference dominates in a dense network.
Tier
A ring of co-channel cells. Tier 1 holds the six nearest; tier 2 sits further out and is normally ignored.

Small N gives every cell a fat slice of the spectrum, but the co-channel cells crowd in and SIR collapses. The escape is sectoring: three directional antennas per site cut the first-tier interferers from six to two, worth about 4.8 dB, which usually buys a step down in N. That gain is not free — each sector trunks its channels separately, so the traffic a cell carries drops unless N drops too, and every handover count rises. The model here is deterministic path loss with one exponent, ideal sector wedges and first-tier interference only; real planning adds shadowing, terrain and antenna front-to-back ratio on top.
