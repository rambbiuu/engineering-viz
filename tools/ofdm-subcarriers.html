OFDM subcarriers and the cyclic prefix

Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) splits one wide channel into many narrow subcarriers, each peak sitting on its neighbours' nulls. A late echo and a mistuned receiver are the two things that break it.

    Worked example, with the current numbers
      An everyday picture
      1. A choir where every singer holds a note that fits a whole number of times into one bar. Over a full bar each other voice averages to nothing, so any one voice can be picked out cleanly.
      2. Let one singer drift off pitch and their note stops cancelling: it smears into everyone else's part. That is intercarrier interference, and the frequency-offset slider creates it.
      Model, and where it is simplified
      1. The channel taps are given zero phase and an exponential power profile rather than random phases, so this is a deterministic delay profile rather than one realisation of Rayleigh fading.
      2. Interference from an echo that overruns the guard is estimated as 2·a2·(τ − Tcp)/Tu summed over the taps that overrun, the standard small-overrun approximation.
      3. The ICI power 1 − sinc2(ε) is the large-N limit, quoted against the total power on the subcarrier, so the true wanted-to-interference ratio is a few tenths of a decibel lower.
      4. The equaliser is given perfect knowledge of the channel; a real receiver estimates it from pilots, and the estimate is worst exactly where the channel is deepest in a notch.
      5. The scene places a single reflector on the ellipse whose extra path length is c·τ. A real channel has many.
      Glossary
      Subcarrier
      One of the N narrow tones that make up an OFDM signal. Each carries its own data symbol.
      Subcarrier spacing Δf
      The frequency step between neighbouring subcarriers, in hertz. It is fixed by the symbol length: Δf = 1/Tu.
      Useful symbol time Tu
      The part of the symbol the receiver integrates over. Every subcarrier fits a whole number of cycles into it, which is what makes them orthogonal.
      Orthogonal
      Two signals are orthogonal when multiplying them together and averaging over one symbol gives exactly zero, so neither disturbs the other.
      sinc
      The function sin(πx)/(πx). It is the spectrum of a rectangular time window, peaks at 1 when x = 0, and is exactly zero at every other whole number.
      Cyclic prefix (CP), or guard interval
      A copy of the end of a symbol pasted onto its front. An echo shorter than the guard then still looks to the receiver like a clean, whole symbol.
      Delay spread
      How long after the first arrival the last usable echo turns up, in microseconds. It is set by the size of the reflecting environment.
      Intersymbol interference (ISI)
      Energy from the previous symbol still arriving inside the current symbol's receive window.
      Intercarrier interference (ICI)
      Energy from neighbouring subcarriers leaking into this one because orthogonality has been broken.
      Frequency offset ε
      The receiver's local oscillator error, measured in units of the subcarrier spacing. ε = 0.1 means the receiver is one tenth of a subcarrier out.
      One-tap equaliser
      Dividing each subcarrier by its own complex channel gain Hk. OFDM's headline advantage: a wideband channel is corrected one narrow tone at a time.
      Peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR)
      The ratio of the highest instantaneous power in a symbol to its mean power, in decibels. It sets how much headroom the transmit amplifier needs.
      Decibel (dB)
      A ratio on a logarithmic scale: 10·log10 of a power ratio. Ten decibels is ten times the power, 3 dB is roughly double.
