OFDM subcarriers and the cyclic prefix

Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM) spaces many narrow subcarriers so every peak lands on every other's null. Two things break it: an echo arriving late, and a receiver tuned slightly off frequency.

New to this? Start with sampling and aliasing and the I/Q constellation, which explain the sinc shape and the constellation dots used below.

An everyday picture
Think of a choir where each singer holds a note that fits a whole number of times into one bar. Over a full bar every other singer's contribution averages to nothing, so you can pick out any one voice cleanly. If one singer drifts slightly off pitch, their note no longer cancels — it smears into everyone else's part. That smearing is intercarrier interference, and the frequency-offset slider below creates it.

      Three trade-offs live in these sliders. Packing more subcarriers into the same bandwidth makes each one narrower and each symbol longer, so a guard of the same percentage covers a longer echo — that is why a channel with a lot of delay spread pushes designers towards large fast Fourier transforms. The guard itself is pure overhead: at 25 % of the symbol you throw away a fifth of your capacity to buy immunity, and the only way to get it back is to shorten the guard until the echoes start bleeding through. And a frequency offset of a few percent of the subcarrier spacing puts a hard ceiling on signal-to-noise ratio that no amount of transmit power can lift, which is why OFDM receivers spend so much effort on synchronisation. Watch the peak-to-average number as you raise N: many subcarriers occasionally line up in phase, and the power amplifier has to stay linear over that peak or it clips and regrows the spectrum into the neighbours' band.

      Where this model is simplified, and it is simplified in four places. The channel taps are given zero phase and an exponential power profile rather than random phases, so this is a deterministic delay profile rather than one realisation of Rayleigh fading — the companion multipath tool is the place for that. The interference from an echo that overruns the guard is estimated as 2·a2·(τ − Tcp)/Tu summed over the taps that overrun, the standard small-overrun approximation; the exact value depends on which data the previous symbol carried. The intercarrier interference power 1 − sinc2(ε) is the large-N limit, and it is quoted relative to the total power falling on the subcarrier rather than to the surviving wanted part, so the true wanted-to-interference ratio is a few tenths of a decibel lower. Finally the equaliser is given perfect knowledge of the channel; a real receiver estimates it from pilots and the estimate is worst exactly where the channel is deepest in a notch.

      Glossary
      Subcarrier
      One of the N narrow tones that make up an OFDM signal. Each carries its own data symbol.
      Subcarrier spacing Δf
      The frequency step between neighbouring subcarriers, in hertz. It is fixed by the symbol length: Δf = 1/Tu.
      Useful symbol time Tu
      The part of the symbol the receiver integrates over. Every subcarrier fits a whole number of cycles into it, which is what makes them orthogonal.
      Orthogonal
      Two signals are orthogonal when multiplying them together and averaging over one symbol gives exactly zero, so neither disturbs the other.
      sinc
      The function sin(πx)/(πx). It is the spectrum of a rectangular time window, peaks at 1 when x = 0, and is exactly zero at every other whole number.
      Cyclic prefix (CP), or guard interval
      A copy of the end of a symbol pasted onto its front. An echo shorter than the guard then still looks to the receiver like a clean, whole symbol.
      Delay spread
      How long after the first arrival the last usable echo turns up, in microseconds. It is set by the size of the reflecting environment.
      Intersymbol interference (ISI)
      Energy from the previous symbol still arriving inside the current symbol's receive window.
      Intercarrier interference (ICI)
      Energy from neighbouring subcarriers leaking into this one because orthogonality has been broken.
      Frequency offset ε
      The receiver's local oscillator error, measured in units of the subcarrier spacing. ε = 0.1 means the receiver is one tenth of a subcarrier out.
      One-tap equaliser
      Dividing each subcarrier by its own complex channel gain Hk. OFDM's headline advantage: a wideband channel is corrected one narrow tone at a time.
      Peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR)
      The ratio of the highest instantaneous power in a symbol to its mean power, in decibels. It sets how much headroom the transmit amplifier needs.
      Decibel (dB)
      A ratio on a logarithmic scale: 10·log10 of a power ratio. Ten decibels is ten times the power, 3 dB is roughly double.
