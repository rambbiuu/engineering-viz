Phased arrays — beam steering, sidelobes and grating lobes

A phased array steers a beam by feeding each element a slightly different phase — nothing rotates. The price is sidelobes, beam broadening off broadside, and extra beams called grating lobes.

If phase, dBi or beamwidth are unfamiliar, start with antenna basics first.

An everyday version. A line of people clapping in step is loudest straight ahead; ask each to clap a fixed fraction of a beat after their neighbour and the loudest direction tilts.

Four trade-offs live in these sliders, and a real array designer argues about all four. More elements buy a narrower beam and about 3 dB of gain per doubling, and cost money and power at every element. Steering away from broadside widens the beam by 1/cos θ0 and loses gain, which is why most arrays are only asked to cover roughly ±60° and are physically turned for anything beyond. Tapering the drive amplitudes buries the sidelobes, and every decibel of sidelobe suppression is paid for in beamwidth and in directivity — Chebyshev is on the good side of that curve, binomial is the extreme case that removes sidelobes entirely and gives up an enormous amount of gain to do it. Spacing is the one with a hard edge: past d/λ = 1/(1+|sin θ0|) a full-strength second beam appears, and a radar cannot tell which of the two a return came from. Half-wavelength spacing is the default everywhere because it is the widest spacing that is safe at every steer angle.

What is modelled and what is not: the plot is the array factor for isotropic elements, so the true pattern of a real array is this multiplied by the pattern of one element, which pulls the far-out lobes down. Directivity is computed exactly for isotropic elements from the element weights and spacing, then multiplied by cos θ0 for the projected aperture — that is the n = 1 scan-loss model; real arrays with directive elements fall closer to cos2.5 θ0, so treat the scan-loss card as the optimistic bound. Mutual coupling between elements, quantised phase shifters and amplitude errors are not modelled.

Glossary
Wavelength λ
The distance from one wave peak to the next. Every spacing here is quoted in wavelengths, so the tool works at any frequency: at 3 GHz, λ = 10 cm, so d = 0.5 λ is 5 cm.
Phase
Where a wave is in its cycle, in degrees. 360° is one whole cycle; a 90° phase shift is a delay of a quarter of a wave.
Array factor
The pattern produced by the positions and feed phases of the elements alone, with each element treated as a point source that radiates equally in all directions.
Broadside
The direction straight out from the face of the array, at right angles to the line of elements. Angles here are measured from broadside.
Main beam and sidelobe
The main beam is the strongest lobe, pointing where you steered. Every other bump is a sidelobe: energy leaking in a direction you did not want, which shows up as interference received from, or transmitted towards, the wrong place.
Grating lobe
A sidelobe that is as strong as the main beam. It appears when the elements are far enough apart that a whole extra cycle of phase fits between neighbours, so the array can no longer tell two directions apart.
HPBW, half-power beamwidth
The angular width of the main beam measured between the two directions where the power has fallen to half of the peak, which is 3 dB down.
Decibel (dB)
A ratio on a log scale: 10 dB is a factor of 10 in power, 3 dB is a factor of 2. Sidelobes are quoted in dB below the main beam.
Directivity, dBi
How much more power the array sends in its best direction than a perfectly omnidirectional antenna would, expressed in dB. The i stands for isotropic, the imaginary antenna that radiates equally everywhere.
Amplitude taper
Driving the middle elements harder than the ones at the ends. It softens the sharp edge of the aperture, which suppresses sidelobes at the cost of a wider beam and less gain.
Scan loss
The gain lost when the beam is steered away from broadside, because the array looks narrower from an angle than it does head on.
