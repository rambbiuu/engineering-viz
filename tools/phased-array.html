Phased arrays — beam steering, sidelobes and grating lobes

A phased array steers its beam by feeding each element a different phase; nothing moves. The price is sidelobes, a wider beam off broadside, and grating lobes when the elements sit too far apart.

An everyday version

The four trade-offs, and what is not modelled

More elements buy a narrower beam and about 3 dB per doubling, and cost money at every element. Steering off broadside widens the beam by 1/cos θ0 and loses gain, which is why most arrays cover roughly ±60° and are turned mechanically beyond that. Tapering buries the sidelobes and pays for it in beamwidth and directivity — Chebyshev sits on the good side of that curve, binomial is the extreme. Spacing has a hard edge: past d/λ = 1/(1+|sin θ0|) a second full-strength beam appears and a radar cannot tell the two apart.

The plot is the array factor for isotropic elements, so a real array's pattern is this multiplied by one element's pattern, which pulls the far-out lobes down. Directivity is computed exactly for isotropic elements from the weights and spacing, then multiplied by cos θ0 for the projected aperture — the n = 1 scan-loss model. Real arrays with directive elements fall closer to cos2.5 θ0, so treat the scan-loss card as the optimistic bound. Mutual coupling, quantised phase shifters and amplitude errors are not modelled.

Glossary
Wavelength λ
The distance from one wave peak to the next. Every spacing here is quoted in wavelengths, so the tool works at any frequency: at 3 GHz, λ = 10 cm, so d = 0.5 λ is 5 cm.
Phase
Where a wave is in its cycle, in degrees. 360° is one whole cycle; a 90° shift is a delay of a quarter of a wave.
Array factor
The pattern produced by the positions and feed phases of the elements alone, each element treated as a point source radiating equally in all directions.
Broadside
The direction straight out from the face of the array, at right angles to the line of elements. Angles here are measured from broadside.
Main beam and sidelobe
The main beam is the strongest lobe, pointing where you steered. Every other bump is a sidelobe: energy leaking towards, or arriving from, a direction you did not want.
Grating lobe
A sidelobe as strong as the main beam. It appears once the elements are far enough apart that a whole extra cycle of phase fits between neighbours.
HPBW, half-power beamwidth
The angular width of the main beam between the two directions where the power has fallen to half of the peak, which is 3 dB down.
Decibel (dB)
A ratio on a log scale: 10 dB is a factor of 10 in power, 3 dB is a factor of 2. Sidelobes are quoted in dB below the main beam.
Directivity, dBi
How much more power the array sends in its best direction than a perfectly omnidirectional antenna would, in dB. The i stands for isotropic.
Amplitude taper
Driving the middle elements harder than the ones at the ends. It softens the edge of the aperture, suppressing sidelobes at the cost of a wider beam and less gain.
Scan loss
The gain lost when the beam is steered off broadside, because the array looks narrower from an angle than it does head on.
