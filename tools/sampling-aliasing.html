Sampling and aliasing

A digital receiver sees only a list of numbers: the wave's height, measured fs times a second. Sample too slowly and a different, slower wave fits the same numbers exactly — the alias.

        The arithmetic, with the current numbers
          Glossary
          Sample
          One measurement of the wave's height, taken at one instant. Between samples the converter knows nothing at all.
          Sample rate, fs
          How many samples are taken each second, in samples per second (Sa/s). Written in hertz too, since it is a rate of repetition.
          Sampling period, Ts
          The time between two samples, equal to 1 ÷ fs, in seconds.
          Nyquist rate
          Twice the highest frequency in the signal, 2f, in Sa/s. The sample rate must be strictly greater than this. Equal is not good enough.
          Nyquist frequency, or the Nyquist limit
          Half the sample rate, fs ÷ 2, in Hz. The highest frequency a given sample rate can represent honestly.
          Aliasing
          A frequency above the Nyquist limit turning up in the samples disguised as a different, lower frequency. The disguise is perfect: the samples of the two are identical numbers.
          Alias frequency, or apparent frequency
          The lower frequency the impostor appears at, in Hz. Computed as the distance from f to the nearest whole multiple of fs.
          Anti-alias filter
          A low-pass filter placed before the converter, removing everything above the Nyquist limit so that nothing can fold. The only real cure, because once a signal is aliased it cannot be separated afterwards.
          Reconstruction filter
          A low-pass filter placed after the converter, cutting at the Nyquist limit, to smooth the output back into one continuous wave and throw away the copies at higher frequencies.
          Zero-order hold
          What a plain digital-to-analogue converter does: hold each sample value steady until the next one arrives, producing a staircase.
          Sinc interpolation
          The ideal way to join the dots. Each sample is replaced by the curve sinc(u) = sin(πu) ÷ (πu), and the curves are added up. It is the exact mathematical equivalent of an ideal low-pass filter at the Nyquist limit.
          Image, or replica
          A copy of the signal's spectrum that sampling creates at every whole multiple of fs. The copies are real: they come out of the converter unless a reconstruction filter removes them.
          Folding
          The way input frequency maps to apparent frequency: the frequency axis is creased at every multiple of half the sample rate and folded back like an accordion.
          Wagon-wheel effect
          Wheels rolling backwards in old films. The camera is a strobe taking 24 pictures a second, and the wheel turns faster than that, so the invented in-between motion runs the wrong way.
          Hold droop
          The amplitude loss a zero-order hold causes, a factor sinc(fa/fs), plus half a sample period of delay. The reconstruction filter smooths the staircase but does not undo the droop.
          Oversampling ratio
          fs divided by the Nyquist rate 2f. A dimensionless number, written with a ×. Anything above 1 works; 1 exactly does not.

          The rule is short and it is strict: fs must be greater than 2f, not equal to it. Step 2 of the tour shows why the equality case fails — with fs = 2f every sample can land on a zero crossing, and a wave whose samples are all zero cannot be told from silence. Two practical consequences follow. First, the only defence is a filter in front of the converter: aliasing is not a processing error you can undo later, because the impostor and the original produce byte-for-byte identical data. Second, real designs do not sit near the limit; audio uses 44.1 kSa/s for a 20 kHz band, about 1.1×, and only gets away with it because the anti-alias filter is very steep, while instrumentation commonly runs 5× or more so that a gentle, cheap filter suffices. One deliberate exception is worth knowing: radio receivers sometimes sample on purpose below the carrier frequency, letting a known band fold down to baseband, which is the same arithmetic used as a tool rather than suffered as a fault. The model here uses a single sine wave and an ideal converter with no quantisation noise and no jitter; add those and the picture gets worse, never better.
