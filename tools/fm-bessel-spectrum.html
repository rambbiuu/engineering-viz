FM sidebands, Bessel functions and Carson's rule

One tone wobbling one carrier creates an endless comb of sidebands whose heights are Bessel functions. Push harder and the power reshuffles along the comb — the carrier itself can vanish — while the total never changes.

1 · The tone, the frequency swing and the wave — how a message of a few volts becomes a carrier that slides back and forth in frequency

  • Top strip: the message, one pure tone m(t) = Am cos(2πfmt). Nothing about it changes when you drag β.
  • Middle strip: where the carrier actually sits at each instant, in kilohertz above or below its resting frequency fc. The shaded band is the full swing, ±Δf. The vertical scale is fixed at the widest swing this tone can produce, so dragging β visibly grows the band.
  • Bottom strip: the transmitted wave. Watch the cycles bunch up where the swing is positive and stretch where it is negative — and watch the two envelope lines never move. That constant envelope is the whole reason FM survives amplitude noise.
  • In FM the swing follows the message itself; in PM it follows the message's slope, which is why the middle strip shifts a quarter period when you switch.

2 · The line spectrum — one tone on one carrier makes an infinite comb of lines, spaced by fm, of height Ac|Jn(β)|

  • Each vertical stem is one sideband at frequency fc + n·fm. Its height is Ac|Jn(β)| volts, computed from the Bessel series, not drawn by hand.
  • Blue stems have a positive Bessel coefficient, orange stems a negative one — a negative coefficient means that line is phase-inverted. Push β past 2.405 and the centre stem turns orange: the carrier has passed through zero and come back inverted.
  • The upper bracket is Carson's rule, the lower one the 1 % rule. Carson is the convenient formula; the 1 % rule counts the sidebands that actually matter. They do not agree, and Carson is the narrower of the two for every β above about 0.6.
  • Total power never moves. Dragging β only shuffles height between stems, which is what the in-band power card is checking.
  • Faint green stems — equivalent AM ghost.

3 · The Bessel functions themselves — every stem height in section 2 is one vertical slice through this picture

  • Six curves, J0 to J5, plotted against β. The vertical line is where you are; the dots on it are the stem heights in section 2, before the Ac scaling.
  • The three dotted lines mark where J0 crosses zero — β = 2.405, 5.520 and 8.654. At those three settings the carrier line disappears completely and all the power lives in the sidebands.
  • Every curve starts at zero except J0, which starts at 1. That is an unmodulated carrier: one line, no sidebands.

Worked example — mono FM broadcast
  1. A station is allowed a peak deviation of Δf = 75 kHz and its audio runs up to fm = 15 kHz.
  2. Modulation index β = Δf / fm = 75 / 15 = 5. It is a pure number: the peak phase swing in radians.
  3. Carson's rule: BW = 2(Δf + fm) = 2(75 + 15) = 180 kHz.
  4. The 1 % rule: the last Bessel coefficient at or above 0.01 is J8(5) = 0.0184, so n′ = 8 and BW = 2 × 8 × 15 = 240 kHz.
  5. The two disagree by 60 kHz, and the regulator sides with neither: broadcast channels are spaced 200 kHz apart, which gives Carson's estimate some room without paying for the full 1 % width.

  7. Single-tone FM output SNR: S0/N0 = 3Ac²β² / (4ηfm) — Ac [V], η one-sided noise PSD [W/Hz], fm [Hz], β dimensionless.
  8. The β² advantage collapses below ≈10 dB input SNR — the capture threshold.
Glossary
Carrier
The steady high-frequency wave the transmitter would send if there were no message. Its frequency is fc.
Sideband
An extra spectral line that modulation creates either side of the carrier. In single-tone FM they appear at fc + n·fm for every whole number n.
Line spectrum
A picture of which frequencies a signal contains and how strong each one is. "Line" because a pure tone occupies a single frequency, drawn as a vertical stem.
Peak deviation Δf
The furthest the carrier's instantaneous frequency strays from fc, in hertz. Set by how loud the message is, not how fast.
Modulation index β
Δf divided by fm. Equivalently the peak phase swing in radians. Dimensionless, and the only thing the sideband heights depend on.
Bessel function Jn(β)
The function that gives the height of the nth sideband. It comes out of writing a wobbling phase as a Fourier series; it is not an approximation.
Carrier null
A value of β where J0 is zero, so the centre line of the spectrum vanishes. Engineers use the first null, β = 2.405, to calibrate a deviation meter.
Bandwidth
The span of positive frequency a signal occupies. FM strictly occupies an infinite span, so both rules here are conventions for "wide enough".
Constant envelope
The property that the wave's peak height never varies. It lets an FM transmitter run its amplifier flat out and lets the receiver throw away amplitude noise.

The lesson is that bandwidth in FM is bought, not given. Set β near zero and the spectrum collapses to a carrier and one sideband pair, occupying 2fm exactly like AM, with none of FM's noise advantage. Raise β and the comb spreads roughly as 2(β+1)fm while the output signal-to-noise ratio climbs as β2 — that is the trade every FM system is built around, and it is why broadcast radio sounds better than AM while using ten times the channel width. Two cautions about what you are looking at. Carson's rule is a convention that captures roughly 98 % of the power, so it under-estimates the true occupied width; the 1 % rule is stricter and is the one to quote when adjacent-channel interference matters. And the bottom strip of section 1 draws the carrier at only 16 times the tone frequency so the squeeze is visible; a real FM carrier is thousands of times higher, and the idea of reading instantaneous frequency off the zero crossings only holds when fm is far below fc.
